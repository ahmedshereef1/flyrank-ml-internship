Assignment: General AI Fluency Track | Week 6
=============================================

Feature built: Contact form on my personal site, wired to my inbox via Netlify Forms.

---
WHAT A BACKEND IS
---

Most websites you visit are actually two things: a frontend and a backend.

The frontend is everything you see — the layout, the text, the buttons. It runs
entirely in your browser. When I send you my index.html file, your browser reads it
and draws the page. No server needed after that initial delivery.

The backend is the part that runs on a server somewhere else, all the time, waiting
for things to happen. When you fill in a form and hit Send, the form data has to go
somewhere — a database, an email inbox, a spreadsheet. That "somewhere" is the
backend. It receives the data, decides what to do with it, and responds.

The reason you need a backend for a contact form is simple: the browser can't send
email. Only a server can. So without a backend, a form is just a box that looks like
it does something but doesn't.

---
WHAT MY FEATURE DOES
---

My site has a contact form with three fields: name, email address, and message.
When someone fills it in and clicks Send, a real message reaches my Gmail inbox
at ahmed.gamal91241@gmail.com.

The backend in this case is Netlify Forms — a service Netlify runs on their servers
for free, specifically for static sites. I didn't write any server code. I added one
attribute to my HTML form tag (`data-netlify="true"`) and Netlify's infrastructure
does the rest.

There is also a honeypot field — a hidden input that real users never see or fill in,
but spam bots often do. If that field has anything in it when the form arrives,
Netlify silently discards the submission. This keeps spam out without a CAPTCHA.

---
HOW THE DATA FLOWS, STEP BY STEP
---

1. Someone types their name, email, and message into the form on my site.

2. They click Send. The browser takes the three field values and packages them
   as form data — essentially a list of key=value pairs, like:
   name=Alex&email=alex@example.com&message=Hello

3. The browser sends that package to Netlify's servers via an HTTP POST request.
   This is the moment the data leaves the browser and goes over the internet.

4. Netlify's server receives the POST. It recognises the form because of the
   `data-netlify="true"` attribute and the hidden `form-name` field I included.
   It checks the honeypot field — if it's empty, the submission is legitimate.

5. Netlify stores the submission in its dashboard (I can view all submissions there)
   and sends an email notification to the address I configured as the site owner:
   ahmed.gamal91241@gmail.com. That email lands in my Gmail inbox.

6. Meanwhile, back on the visitor's browser: the JavaScript I wrote intercepts the
   form submit, sends it via fetch() instead of a full page reload, waits for
   Netlify to confirm success (HTTP 200), then hides the form and shows a
   "Message sent" confirmation. If something goes wrong, it re-enables the Send
   button and tells the visitor to email me directly instead.

---
WHY THIS APPROACH
---

The alternative would be to write my own server — a Node.js or Python app that
receives the form, calls an email API, and sends the message. That works, but it
requires hosting a server, keeping it running, handling errors, managing API keys,
and paying for compute. For a contact form on a personal site, that's 20x more
complexity than the job needs.

Netlify Forms offloads all of that to infrastructure that already exists and is
free at this scale. The tradeoff is that I'm depending on Netlify's service rather
than owning the backend myself. For this use case — a personal portfolio with
occasional contact form submissions — that tradeoff is correct.

The one thing I had to understand to make this work: the form data doesn't go
directly to my inbox. It goes to Netlify's server first, and Netlify's server
emails me. The email I receive is sent by Netlify on behalf of the form submission,
not by the visitor's browser. That middle step is what "the backend" means here.
